# Forks and merges without another namespace's fold

This note describes how a fork and a merge use another namespace's unfolded [WAL tail](../specs/glossary.md#wal-tail) without making that namespace [fold](../specs/glossary.md#fold). A [fork](../specs/glossary.md#fork) of a namespace's head folds the source's tail into the fork's own first manifests, and the source publishes nothing. A [merge](../specs/glossary.md#merge) writes the fork's unfolded bytes to content objects before it admits them, as a copy does, and the fork publishes nothing. So each namespace's manifests are published by that namespace's own operations.

In this note, "the [source](../specs/glossary.md#source)" is the namespace that a fork is made from, "main" is the namespace that a merge writes, and "the fork" is the namespace whose commits a merge applies. The examples use a namespace called `main`, whose current manifest 46 has [folded head](../specs/glossary.md#folded-head) 400 and `folded_wal_no` 80, and whose WAL objects 81 to 83 contain commits 401 to 418. The examples also use `sandbox`, a fork of `main`'s head made at sequence 418.

## The problem

This section describes why forks and merges fold another namespace today.

A fork of the source's head first folds the source's WAL tail ([Creating and verifying a snapshot](../specs/format.md#82-creating-and-verifying-a-snapshot), step 1, and [`POST /forks`](../specs/api.md#613-post-forks), step 1). The fork's starting state has to be a manifest, because the fork's manifest 1 copies the source's runs and folded head, and the fork [snapshot](../specs/glossary.md#snapshot) keeps the source manifest that the fork reads through. So in the example, a fork of `main` makes `main` fold WAL objects 81 to 83 and publish manifest 47 before the fork writes anything of its own. The fork's call publishes a manifest of the source, and its fold competes with the source's own writer (the process that commits to the source), which folds the same tail on its own schedule.

A merge first folds the fork ([Merging a fork](merge.md#merging), step 7, and [`POST /merges`](../specs/api.md#614-post-merges), step 6). A merge's commit refers to the fork's content by reference, and [Cross-namespace copies and moves](../specs/format.md#97-cross-namespace-copies-and-moves) requires a layout for each reference. A revision whose bytes are still pieces in the fork's WAL doesn't have one. The fold is the largest part of a merge's time, and it runs on every merge and every [rebase](../specs/glossary.md#rebase) replay commit.

In both cases, one namespace's operation publishes a manifest of another namespace.

## The design

This section describes the fork, the merge, the rule they share for writing another member's pieces, and what stays the same.

### A fork folds the source's tail into itself

A fork of the source's head reads the source's current manifest and WAL tail, which is the view that any read of the source uses. The [fork point](../specs/glossary.md#fork-point) is the head of that view. Instead of making the source fold the tail, the fork folds the tail's commits into a run of its own, under its own prefix, and keeps the result with a snapshot on itself.

A "fork point snapshot" is a fork [snapshot](../specs/glossary.md#snapshot) that a fork keeps on its own manifest at the fork point. That manifest lists the source's runs and the fork's run of the source's tail, so it describes the source's state at the fork point under the fork's namespace ID. The fork point snapshot keeps that manifest, its segments, and the content its layouts list until the fork is retired. Every fork has one, including a fork of a user snapshot, so a client reads every fork's starting state the same way.

To fork the source's head, LoonFS:

1. Finds the target, as today.
2. Reads the source's current manifest and its WAL tail, with the checks of a discovery ([Resolving the metadata basis](../specs/format.md#41-resolving-the-metadata-basis) and [Replaying the visible WAL](../specs/format.md#42-replaying-the-visible-wal)). The fork point is the head of that view. If a commit in the tail is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS` (11 hours and 45 minutes, the limit that a merge applies to the fork's tail, described below), LoonFS folds the source first, through the source's own fold path, and reads again.
3. Writes the temporary snapshot on the source's current manifest, without a fold, and checks that the manifest is still current, as [Creating and verifying a snapshot](../specs/format.md#82-creating-and-verifying-a-snapshot) checks a snapshot of the head. If a newer manifest is current, LoonFS starts again at step 2.
4. Publishes manifest 1 with the status `initializing`, built from the source manifest. Its head is the source manifest's folded head, and its retention floor is the source manifest's floor. Its fork basis records the fork point (`fork_point_seq`), the fork snapshot's ID, and the fork point snapshot's ID.
5. If the tail has a commit, folds the tail's records ([Publishing a manifest](../specs/format.md#72-publishing-a-manifest)). It writes each chain's pending pieces as one extent under the pool's prefix (see [Writing another member's pieces](#writing-another-members-pieces)), writes a metadata segment under its own prefix for each family that the tail's commits touched, and publishes manifest 2, still `initializing`. Manifest 2 lists the source manifest's runs followed by the new run. Its head is the fork point, its floor is manifest 1's floor, and its `folded_wal_no` is still 0.
6. Writes the fork point snapshot on its newest manifest (manifest 2, or manifest 1 when the tail had no commit) with put-if-absent, under the ID that manifest 1 records.
7. Writes the fork snapshot on the source manifest and the member entry, and checks the source and the temporary snapshot, as today.
8. Publishes the next manifest with the status `active`. It's the previous manifest with its retention floor raised to the fork point.

In the example, `sandbox` reads `main`'s manifest 46 and WAL objects 81 to 83, takes its temporary snapshot on manifest 46, and publishes its manifest 1 from manifest 46, at head 400. It writes span objects for the tail's pieces and its own segments for commits 401 to 418, and publishes its manifest 2 at head 418, listing `main`'s runs and its new run. It writes its fork point snapshot on its manifest 2, its fork snapshot on `main`'s manifest 46, and its member entry, and publishes its manifest 3, active, with floor 418. `main` publishes nothing. `sandbox`'s first commit is 419.

A fork of a user snapshot has no tail. Its fork point is the snapshot's head, it publishes no manifest 2, and its fork point snapshot keeps its manifest 1. So does a fork of a head whose tail has only fences and floor objects, because a tail with no commit changes nothing that manifest 2 would record.

The fork's activity counters stay at 0 through its fold of the source's tail, because those commits are the source's activity ([Statistics](../specs/format.md#74-statistics)).

Why does the fork fold the tail and not the source? The fold's writes have to happen somewhere, and if they happen in the fork, no operation publishes another namespace's manifest. The source's writer and maintainer fold the source's tail on their own schedule, and a fork never races them, except when the source's tail has a commit older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`. Then the fork folds the source first, for the reason given under the merge below: the content that the fork takes from the tail is protected only while the tail is younger than that limit.

Why does manifest 2 keep manifest 1's floor instead of the floor of the source's view? A floor object in the source's tail raises the view's floor, and a fold of the source would record the raised floor. A call that finishes a fork from manifest 1 re-reads the tail through the fork point, and a floor object after the last commit isn't in what it reads, so if manifest 2 applied floor objects, two calls could publish two different manifests 2. Keeping manifest 1's floor makes manifest 2 a function of the tail's commits alone. The active manifest raises the floor to the fork point in any case, and a read through the fork point snapshot still finds every row below the source's raised floor, because the fork's manifests list the source's segments.

Why a snapshot on the fork itself? Today the fork snapshot on the source keeps the source's manifest at the fork point, and three readers depend on that view: a client that reads a file as it was at the fork point, a rebase's conflict classification and its resolver, and grep's first step in a fork. When the source has an unfolded tail, it no longer publishes a manifest at the fork point, so the only manifest at the fork point is one that the fork publishes. The fork's own retention can't remove that manifest's history, because the fork point snapshot keeps the manifest as it was published, the same way the fork snapshot on the source keeps the source's manifest. The fork snapshot on the source stays, because the fork's manifests list the source's segments.

Why are there two initializing manifests, and why is the floor raised last? The fork point snapshot has to exist before the fork is active. Otherwise a call that stopped right after activation would leave an active fork with no fork point snapshot, and no call would finish it. So the fork point snapshot is written on an initializing manifest, and the active manifest follows it. That initializing manifest keeps the source's floor, so a read through the fork point snapshot sees the source's history at the fork point exactly as a read of the source would (its change feed, for example, and its revisions above the source's floor). The active manifest raises the floor to the fork point, because a fork's floor is its fork point ([Advancing the retention floor](../specs/format.md#101-advancing-the-retention-floor)). The fork's WAL has no commits before the namespace is active, so no floor object is needed to order the raise against a restore.

What keeps the source's tail while the fork folds it? The fork reads the tail's records in step 2, and the discovery checks that no fold replaced the source manifest while the tail was read, so the records are the source's history from the manifest's folded head through the fork point. A fold or a collection of the source after that read doesn't change what the fork writes. The temporary snapshot keeps the source manifest and its segments until the fork snapshot is verified, as today.

What keeps the content that the fork's manifest refers to? [Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots) counts every manifest that a member's snapshots keep as one of the member's layout views. The fork point snapshot keeps the fork's manifest at the fork point, so the content at the fork point stays for the fork's life, as it does today through the source's manifest. That includes the content of revisions that a later commit in the source's tail replaced, because the fork's run has the tail's whole history above the source's floor. Before its member entry is written, the fork has no roots, and from then on the manifest that its fork point snapshot keeps is one. A collection that listed the pool's members before the member entry was written doesn't read the fork, and a span object that the fork's fold writes can already exist, because the source's validation wrote the same span for a copy while the pieces were in the source's tail, and a write that finds its key occupied doesn't make the object younger. So the fork's extents aren't protected by their age. They're protected because every reference that the fork takes from the tail was in the source's WAL tail when the fork read it, and the fork takes the tail only while no commit in it is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`. A retention advance of the source passes the tail's commits only after any collection that missed the fork has read the source (the inequality in [Publication and collection timing](../specs/format.md#c1-publication-and-collection-timing)), so that collection finds each reference in the source's current manifest or WAL tail, as the paragraph on a fork created during a collection in [Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots) describes.

A call that finishes an initializing fork ([Finishing an initializing namespace](../specs/format.md#finishing-an-initializing-namespace)) continues from the newest manifest it finds. If manifest 1 is the newest and the fork point is above its head, the call folds the tail again, from the source's WAL objects above the source manifest's `folded_wal_no` through the object whose head is the fork point. The tail has a commit exactly when the fork point is above manifest 1's head, so the rule is exact, and the manifest 2 that the call publishes is the one that the first call would have published. It reads them whether or not the source has folded them since. If one is gone, because a fold covered it and a collection deleted it, the call returns `snapshot_not_found`, which is the same error, with the same outcome for the namespace, as a finishing call makes after the temporary snapshot expired. If two calls fold at once, the one whose manifest 2 put loses continues from the winner's manifest 2, and garbage collection deletes the loser's segments and extents under the age rules.

### A merge writes the fork's pending pieces, as a copy does

[File contents and ownership](../specs/format.md#15-file-contents-and-ownership) and [Operations and WAL deltas](../specs/format.md#66-operations-and-wal-deltas) already say that before LoonFS admits a copy, a restore, or a new chain, validation writes the pending [pieces](../specs/glossary.md#piece) that the layout needs to content objects, the same way a fold does. A merge admits references from another member of the pool, so it's a copy across namespaces, and it does the same.

The merge plans from the fork's head view, which is the fork's current manifest and WAL tail, the view that every read of the fork uses. For each reference in the merge whose bytes are partly or wholly pieces in the fork's tail, the merge writes those pieces as the chain's next extent (see [Writing another member's pieces](#writing-another-members-pieces)), and includes the resulting layout in its commit. So every reference in a merge's commit has a layout, as today. A merge still includes no inline content and appends no bytes, and rule 1 of [WAL records](../specs/format.md#a5-wal-records) doesn't change. The planner admits each reference with the existing layout proof (`PreparedContent::for_pool_member`), whose layout comes from the fork's view and the extents that the merge just wrote. The fork's own later fold writes its own extent for the same pieces.

In the example, `sandbox` creates `/notes.txt` with 10 KiB at sequence 419, appends 2 KiB at 420, and merges before it folds. The merge writes the 12 KiB as a span object of `sandbox`'s chain, and the commit in `main` includes a layout with that extent, which records `sandbox` as the chain's creator.

The merge still takes a snapshot of the fork while it plans, with the same 5-minute expiry, but on the fork's current manifest and without a fold. The snapshot protects a revision that the merge includes and that the fork replaced before the plan, which happens when the merge's range ends below the fork's head. The replacing commit can already be older than the grace window, so without the snapshot, a retention advance and a base compaction of the fork could remove the revision and its layout row right after the plan ([Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots)).

A snapshot keeps a manifest and not a WAL tail, so it doesn't cover a revision that the plan reads from the fork's tail. That case needs an old tail. Suppose the fork's tail includes a revision in the merge's range whose content has a layout (an upload, for example), and a later commit in the tail replaced it before the plan. If the replacing commit is older than the grace window less the other terms of the inequality in [Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots), a fold, a retention advance, and a base compaction of the fork can remove the revision's layout row before a collection reads the fork, when that collection read main before the merge's commit. So a merge plans from the fork's head view only while no commit in the fork's tail is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`:

```text
GC_GRACE_WINDOW_MS - (POOL_ROOTS_READ_BUDGET_MS + GC_SAFETY_MARGIN_MS
    + OPERATION_SNAPSHOT_TTL_MS + PROVIDER_PUBLICATION_REQUEST_BOUND_MS)
```

With the current values, the limit is 11 hours and 45 minutes. If the fork's tail has an older commit, the merge folds the fork first, as today, and plans from the manifest that the fold published. The reference implementation folds a tail that has been idle for 15 minutes ([Recovery material and maintenance policy](../specs/format.md#73-recovery-material-and-maintenance-policy)), so a tail is normally far younger than the limit, and the fold is a backstop that rarely runs.

A retry of a merge with the same commit ID and merge record is a replay, because a merge's fingerprint covers its merge record in place of its operations ([Commit identity and retries](../specs/format.md#65-commit-identity-and-retries)). A retry whose plan writes the same pieces again finds the keys occupied with the same bytes. A rebase's replay commit is a merge into the rebase's new fork, so it takes the same path.

### Writing another member's pieces

A fork that folds its source's tail, and a merge that admits the fork's references, both write the pending pieces of a chain that another member of the pool created. One rule covers both.

When a namespace writes the pending pieces of a chain that another member created, the layout that it writes records that member as the [chain's creator](../specs/glossary.md#chain-creator), and it writes the pieces as one extent, which is never combined with the chain's existing extents. The creator comes from the chain's existing layout, or, for a chain that started in the tail, from rule 1 of [WAL records](../specs/format.md#a5-wal-records) applied to the WAL object that started the chain. That WAL object belongs to the creator, because a namespace's WAL includes pieces only for chains that the namespace created, so the creator is exact. The definition of a chain's creator, and the rule that only the creator extends a chain in place ([File contents and ownership](../specs/format.md#15-file-contents-and-ownership)), don't change.

A fold and validation's write of pending pieces share one plan, which applies the doubling rule to the chain's own extents (its earlier extents under its own content ID). A namespace that isn't the chain's creator treats those extents as shared base extents, which a fold never combines. So it writes only the pieces, and it never copies the creator's earlier bytes into an object that only its own layout lists.

The existing rule for an occupied key ([Publishing a manifest](../specs/format.md#72-publishing-a-manifest)) makes two writers of the same pieces safe. A span object's key is the chain's content ID followed by the extent's start and end, and only the creator extends a chain, so every write at one key stores the same bytes of the same chain. If two writers build the same extent, the second finds the key occupied with the same stored checksum, and its write succeeds. If they build different extents, because the creator combined earlier extents and the other writer didn't, they write different keys, and both layouts describe the same bytes.

For example, `main` started the chain of `/build.log` in commit 410. `sandbox`'s fold of `main`'s tail writes the chain's pieces as `{content_id}-00000000000000000000-00000000000000004096`, and `main`'s own fold later writes the same key with the same bytes, which succeeds. If `main` had already folded an earlier extent of the chain and combined it under the doubling rule, `main` would write a longer span, `sandbox` would have written a span of the tail's pieces alone, and the pool would store those 4 KiB twice.

### What stays the same

A content ID never changes. A reference identifies the same object in every member of a pool, and a merge compares the fork's rows with main's rows by reference, so if a fork or a merge assigned content new IDs, inherited revisions would look like changes. Nothing in this design copies content. The only bytes it writes are pieces written to extents, which is what a fold does.

The design adds one snapshot for each fork, on the fork itself. It adds no pin, no new kind of root, and no copy of a WAL object. The fork point snapshot is a fork snapshot, and the manifest that it keeps is a layout view under the existing rule for the manifests that a member's snapshots keep.

Two properties hold everywhere, as they do today. The pieces in a namespace's WAL belong to chains that the namespace created, so replay's inference of a tail chain's creator stays exact. Every reference that a namespace admits from another member has a layout, so a merge's commit includes no bytes, and its size doesn't depend on how much of the fork is folded.

## Reading the fork point

A client reads a fork as it was at the fork point with `snapshot_id` set to the fork's fork point snapshot, which the namespace object's `fork_basis.fork_point_snapshot_id` identifies. The read is a read of the fork's own namespace, and it returns the source's state at the fork point, because the snapshot's manifest lists the source's runs and the fork's run of the source's tail, with the source's floor. The fork snapshot on the source is no longer part of the API, because nothing reads the source through it, and only the fork's retirement uses its ID.

A rebase reads main at the new fork's fork point through the new fork's fork point snapshot, both for its conflict classification (main's commit rows, the base version, and the subtree diff up to that point) and for the resolver's read of each conflict's base. The snapshot's manifest has main's floor, which the rebase checks against the replayed fork's fork point before it creates the new fork, so the base revision and main's history between the two fork points are in that manifest.

Grep's first step in a fork reads the gap between the inherited index's watermark and the fork point through the fork point snapshot. The snapshot's manifest lists the source's runs and the fork's run, so one view has every commit from the source's floor through the fork point, and the step reads nothing from the source.

## Costs

This section lists what the design costs, compared with folding the other namespace first.

- A fork of a head with an unfolded tail makes three more requests than today. The fold's writes move from the source's prefix to the fork's, and the source's manifest put becomes the fork's manifest 2 put. The fork adds the fork point snapshot's put and one more manifest put with its boundary read. A fork of a user snapshot, or of a head with no tail, adds the fork point snapshot's put. (A runtime test counts 15 requests for a fork on a warm handle, before the fold's own reads and writes.)
- The source's tail is folded twice over time, once by the fork when it's created and once by the source on its own schedule. So one tail's metadata segments are stored twice, and its pieces' extents can be stored twice. Neither duplicate is larger than one tail.
- The gain is ownership, not latency. The source publishes nothing for a fork, its fold schedule is its own, and no operation publishes another namespace's manifest.
- A merge writes one extent for each chain with pending pieces in its range, and it doesn't write the fork's segments or publish a manifest of the fork. The fork's later fold writes the same pieces again, to the same span object unless it combines them with the chain's earlier extents. Measured on an in-memory store with a release build, a merge's fold of a fork that has 1,000 unfolded files of 1 KiB writes 1,000 content objects, 7 metadata segments, and 1 manifest, and building the segments takes 51 ms of the fold's 81 ms. With 100 files it writes 100 content objects and the same 8 metadata objects. So the design saves the 8 metadata writes and most of the fold's processor time on every merge, and the content writes stay, because they are the fold's own work done earlier. The content writes go out 32 at a time, so against an object store a merge of 1,000 unfolded small files still waits for about 32 rounds of writes, and a merge of files that already have layouts waits for none.
- A merge whose fork has a tail commit older than 11 hours and 45 minutes folds the fork first, as every merge does today, and a fork of a head whose tail has such a commit folds the source first, as every fork does today.
- Every fork keeps two fork snapshot records until its retirement, one on its source and one on itself, and its retirement deletes both.

## What the format and API specs change

In the storage format:

- [Replaying the visible WAL](../specs/format.md#42-replaying-the-visible-wal) drops the sentences that a fork folds the source's WAL tail first and that the runtime's view of the source moves onto that fold's manifest.
- [Merges](../specs/format.md#merges) reads the fork point from `fork_basis.fork_point_seq`, in place of `fork_basis.manifest.head_seq`, in the range rule and in the rebase substitutions.
- [Publishing a manifest](../specs/format.md#72-publishing-a-manifest) changes the fork column of the first manifest's table (manifest 1's floor is the source manifest's floor, and the table describes manifest 2 and the active manifest). It replaces the sentence that a layout's creator is the folding namespace with the rule for another member's chain, adds that rule to the candidate steps, and says that a fork's active manifest is the one publication other than a fold that raises a floor, and that manifest 2 keeps manifest 1's floor.
- [Records and owners](../specs/format.md#81-records-and-owners) says that a fork has two fork snapshots, one on its source that keeps the source manifest and one on itself that keeps its manifest at the fork point, and that the temporary snapshot is taken on the source's current manifest.
- [Creating and verifying a snapshot](../specs/format.md#82-creating-and-verifying-a-snapshot) adds a snapshot of the current manifest without a fold, which a fork of the head and a merge's plan take.
- [Reads, deletion, and expiry](../specs/format.md#83-reads-deletion-and-expiry) says that a read of a fork's starting state goes through the fork's own fork point snapshot.
- [Forking a namespace](../specs/format.md#92-forking-a-namespace) changes its steps, its description of manifest 1, its sentence that a fork copies no metadata segments, its count of object writes and its example, its paragraph on the temporary snapshot, and its finishing rules.
- [Retirement](../specs/format.md#95-retirement) and [Snapshot cleanup](../specs/format.md#117-snapshot-cleanup) say that a namespace's own fork point snapshot doesn't block its retirement, that its collection keeps that snapshot until the retirement, and that the retirement deletes both of the fork's snapshot records.
- [Cross-namespace copies and moves](../specs/format.md#97-cross-namespace-copies-and-moves) says that a merge writes the fork's pending pieces first, as a copy does, that its snapshot is taken without a fold, and that a merge whose fork has a tail commit older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS` folds the fork first.
- [Advancing the retention floor](../specs/format.md#101-advancing-the-retention-floor) says that a fork's active manifest sets its floor to the fork point.
- [Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots) changes the replaced-revision paragraph (the merge's snapshot is of the fork's current manifest, with the limit on the tail's age) and the paragraph on a fork created during a collection (the tail's references are protected by the same limit on the source's tail, not by the age of the fork's extents).
- [Control and manifest payloads](../specs/format.md#a4-control-and-manifest-payloads) adds `fork_point_seq` and `fork_point_snapshot_id` to the fork basis.
- [Index lifecycle](../specs/format.md#d2-index-lifecycle) reads a fork's gap through the fork point snapshot, and drops the case of a source or a fork snapshot that's gone.

The manifest validators change with the first manifest's table. A fork's manifest 1 records its source manifest's floor, where today its floor has to equal its head (`ensure_first_manifest`). A fork's initializing manifest 1 may be followed by an initializing manifest 2 that adds one run at the fork point and raises the head and the next run number, and a fork's active manifest is the previous manifest activated with its floor raised to the fork point (`ensure_followed_by`, which today accepts only an activation that changes nothing but the status). Nothing ties an advance of the head to the folded WAL number, so a manifest at head 418 with `folded_wal_no` 0 passes the run checks as they are.

In the API:

- [`POST /forks`](../specs/api.md#613-post-forks) replaces its first step with the fold of the source's tail into the fork, keeps the source's fold for a tail with a commit older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`, and adds the fork point snapshot.
- [`POST /merges`](../specs/api.md#614-post-merges) replaces the fold in step 6 with the snapshot of the fork's current manifest and the limit on the tail's age, and says that the merge writes the fork's pending pieces before it admits them.
- [`POST /replays`](../specs/api.md#617-post-replays) changes step 4 to match, and its conflict report says that a client reads the base through the new fork's fork point snapshot.
- [Snapshots](../specs/api.md#snapshots) says that a read of a fork accepts the fork's fork point snapshot, and the API's `fork_basis` object exposes `fork_point_snapshot_id` in place of `source_snapshot_id` (the format's fork basis records both snapshot IDs, and only the fork's retirement uses the source-side one).
- The glossary's [Fork](../specs/glossary.md#fork), [Fork point](../specs/glossary.md#fork-point), and [Snapshot](../specs/glossary.md#snapshot) entries change to match.

[File contents and ownership](../specs/format.md#15-file-contents-and-ownership), [Operations and WAL deltas](../specs/format.md#66-operations-and-wal-deltas), [WAL records](../specs/format.md#a5-wal-records), and [Required operations](../specs/format.md#31-required-operations) don't change.

## Alternatives considered

- **Folding the other namespace first.** This is the current design. Each fork of a head with an unfolded tail makes the source publish a manifest, and each merge makes the fork publish one. A fork's call races the source's writer on the source's fold.
- **Copying the source's WAL records into the fork's WAL.** The fork writes the source's tail records as its own first WAL objects, and reads them like any tail. Replay then has to accept WAL objects that record another namespace's ID and a writer epoch of 0, and the fork needs a floor object to reach its floor. The fork's manifests never describe the fork point, so keeping the fork point readable needs a retention exception in the fork or a new root for the copied objects. A namespace's WAL would also include pieces of chains that another member created.
- **Including the fork's pieces in the merge's commit.** The merge includes the fork's unfolded bytes as inline content, and main's fold writes them. Main's view would then take a chain's creator from the merge record, which identifies the wrong namespace after a rebase, unless the WAL format adds a creator field. A later merge of an appended chain needs a rule to include only the new pieces, the planner needs a proof that includes pieces under an existing content ID, and the 4 MiB limit on inline content in one WAL object splits merges of large unfolded forks. A namespace would also admit references from another member without a layout.
- **Reading the source's WAL objects in place.** The fork snapshot keeps a range of the source's WAL objects as well as its manifest, and the fork replays them from the source's prefix. The fork's view then has a tail from another namespace, the snapshot record lists WAL objects, and the source's collection has to keep WAL objects that its own manifest has folded.
- **Copying the tail's content under new IDs.** The fork writes the tail's pieces under content IDs that it creates. Every inherited revision then has a reference that differs from main's, so the next merge applies inherited revisions as changes or reports them as conflicts.
- **Asking the source's writer to fold at the fork point.** The source still folds, the fork depends on the source's writer being reachable, and a fold covers the tail that it finds, so it publishes a manifest at the fork point only if no commit is added in between.
- **A new snapshot owner for the fork point snapshot.** The snapshot keeps a manifest until the fork's retirement, like a fork snapshot, and readers and collections already handle fork snapshots, so a new owner would add a third set of rules for the same lifetime.
