# Forks and merges without another namespace's fold

This note describes how a fork and a merge use another namespace's unfolded [WAL tail](../specs/glossary.md#wal-tail) without making that namespace [fold](../specs/glossary.md#fold). A [fork](../specs/glossary.md#fork) of a namespace's head folds the source's tail into the fork's own manifest 2, so the source publishes nothing. A [merge](../specs/glossary.md#merge) writes the fork's pending [pieces](../specs/glossary.md#piece) to content objects before it admits them, as a copy does, so the fork publishes nothing. The exception is a tail with a commit older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS` (11 hours and 45 minutes). The fork or the merge then folds the other namespace first, as both do today.

In this note, "the [source](../specs/glossary.md#source)" is the namespace that a fork is made from, "main" is the namespace that a merge writes, and "the fork" is the namespace whose commits a merge applies. The examples use a namespace called `main`, whose current manifest 46 has [folded head](../specs/glossary.md#folded-head) 400 and `folded_wal_no` 80, and whose WAL objects 81 to 83 contain commits 401 to 418. The examples also use `sandbox`, a fork of `main`'s head made at sequence 418.

## The problem

This section describes why forks and merges fold another namespace today.

A fork of the source's head first folds the source's WAL tail ([Creating and verifying a snapshot](../specs/format.md#82-creating-and-verifying-a-snapshot), step 1, and [`POST /forks`](../specs/api.md#613-post-forks), step 1). The fork's starting state has to be a manifest, because the fork's manifest 1 copies the source's runs and folded head, and the fork [snapshot](../specs/glossary.md#snapshot) keeps the source manifest that the fork reads through. So in the example, a fork of `main` makes `main` fold WAL objects 81 to 83 and publish manifest 47 before the fork writes anything of its own. The fork's call publishes a manifest of the source, and its fold competes with the source's own writer (the process that commits to the source), which folds the same tail on its own schedule.

A merge first folds the fork ([Merging](merge.md#merging) in the merge design note, step 7, and [`POST /merges`](../specs/api.md#614-post-merges), step 6). A merge's commit refers to the fork's content by reference, and [Cross-namespace copies and moves](../specs/format.md#97-cross-namespace-copies-and-moves) requires a layout for each reference. A revision whose bytes are still pieces in the fork's WAL doesn't have one. The fold is the largest part of a merge's time, and it runs on every merge and every [rebase](../specs/glossary.md#rebase) replay commit.

In both cases, one namespace's operation publishes a manifest of another namespace.

## The design

This section describes the fork, the merge, the rule they share for writing another member's pieces, and what stays the same.

### A fork folds the source's tail into itself

A fork of the source's head reads the source's current manifest and WAL tail, which is the view that any read of the source uses. The [fork point](../specs/glossary.md#fork-point) is the head of that view. Instead of making the source fold the tail, the fork folds the tail's commits into a run of its own, under its own prefix, and keeps the result with a snapshot on itself. This works like a fold (it writes the tail's commits into metadata segments and publishes a manifest that lists them), but the WAL objects are the source's, the manifest is the fork's, and the fork's `folded_wal_no` and retention floor don't change.

A "fork point snapshot" is a fork [snapshot](../specs/glossary.md#snapshot) that a fork keeps on its own manifest at the fork point. That manifest lists the source's runs and the fork's run of the source's tail, so it describes the source's state at the fork point under the fork's namespace ID. The fork point snapshot keeps that manifest, its segments, and the content its layouts list until the fork is retired. Every fork has one, including a fork of a user snapshot, so a client reads every fork's starting state the same way.

To fork the source's head, LoonFS:

1. Finds the target, as today.
2. Reads the source's current manifest and its WAL tail, with the checks of a discovery ([Resolving the metadata basis](../specs/format.md#41-resolving-the-metadata-basis) and [Replaying the visible WAL](../specs/format.md#42-replaying-the-visible-wal)), and the fork point is the head of that view. LoonFS then checks, by the `committed_at_ms` of each commit, whether a commit in that view's tail (the tail that the fork folds) is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS` (11 hours and 45 minutes, the same limit that a merge applies to the fork's tail, as described in [A merge writes the fork's pending pieces, as a copy does](#a-merge-writes-the-forks-pending-pieces-as-a-copy-does)), measured at the time that the fork call started. If a commit is older, LoonFS writes nothing for the fork yet. It folds the source with the same fold that the source's writer runs, and then reads the source's view again and checks it again. The reference implementation makes up to `MAX_CONTENTION_ATTEMPTS` (8) of these folds and reads, and then returns `store_contention`.
3. Writes the temporary snapshot on the source's current manifest, without a fold, and checks that the manifest is still current, as [Creating and verifying a snapshot](../specs/format.md#82-creating-and-verifying-a-snapshot) checks a snapshot of the head. If a newer manifest is current, LoonFS reads the source's manifest and WAL tail again, checks the tail's age again, and takes a new temporary snapshot.
4. Publishes manifest 1 with the status `initializing`, built from the source manifest. Its folded head is the source manifest's folded head, and its retention floor is the source manifest's floor. Its fork basis records the fork point (`fork_point_seq`), the fork snapshot's ID, and the fork point snapshot's ID.
5. If the tail has a commit, folds the tail's commits, not its floor objects ([Publishing a manifest](../specs/format.md#72-publishing-a-manifest)). It writes each chain's pending pieces as one extent under the pool's prefix (see [Writing another member's pieces](#writing-another-members-pieces)), writes a metadata segment under its own prefix for each family that the tail's commits touched, and publishes manifest 2, still `initializing`. Manifest 2 lists the source manifest's runs followed by the new run. Its folded head is the fork point, its floor is manifest 1's floor, and its `folded_wal_no` is still 0.
6. Checks `METADATA_PUBLICATION_BUDGET_MS` (15 minutes), as the fork snapshot's write does, and writes the fork point snapshot on its newest manifest (manifest 2, or manifest 1 when the tail had no commit) with put-if-absent, under the ID that manifest 1 records.
7. Writes the member entry and then the fork snapshot on the source manifest, and checks the source and the temporary snapshot, as today.
8. Publishes the next manifest with the status `active`. It's the previous manifest with its retention floor raised to the fork point.

In the example, `sandbox` reads `main`'s manifest 46 and WAL objects 81 to 83, takes its temporary snapshot on manifest 46, and publishes its manifest 1 from manifest 46, at folded head 400. It writes span objects for the tail's pieces and its own segments for commits 401 to 418, and publishes its manifest 2 at folded head 418, listing `main`'s runs and its new run. It writes its fork point snapshot on its manifest 2, its member entry, and its fork snapshot on `main`'s manifest 46, and publishes its manifest 3, active, with floor 418. `main` publishes nothing. `sandbox`'s first commit is 419.

A fork of a user snapshot doesn't read the source's WAL tail. Its fork point is the snapshot's head, it publishes no manifest 2, and its fork point snapshot keeps its manifest 1. A fork of a head whose tail has only fences and floor objects also publishes no manifest 2, and its fork point snapshot keeps its manifest 1, because manifest 2 records only the tail's commits.

A fork that folds a tail (a fork whose fork point is above the head of the source manifest that the temporary snapshot keeps) has a deadline. It has to publish its active manifest within `OPERATION_SNAPSHOT_TTL_MS` (5 minutes) of the temporary snapshot's creation time (`created_at_ms`). Before each write after step 3, and before the check after the fork snapshot's write in step 7, LoonFS checks that deadline. A call that's past the deadline writes nothing more and returns `snapshot_not_found`, as it does after the temporary snapshot ends. Extending the temporary snapshot doesn't move the deadline, because the snapshot's creation time can't change. A fork that folds no tail, whether of the head or of a user snapshot, has no deadline, because every reference that it starts with is in the source manifest that its snapshot keeps. In the example, `sandbox` folds commits 401 to 418, so it has to publish its manifest 3 within 5 minutes of its temporary snapshot's creation.

The fork's activity counters stay at 0 through its fold of the source's tail, because those commits are the source's activity ([Statistics](../specs/format.md#74-statistics)).

Why does the fork fold the tail and not the source? The fold's writes have to happen somewhere, and if they happen in the fork, the fork's call publishes no manifest of the source. The source's writer and maintainer fold the source's tail on their own schedule, and a fork doesn't race them unless the source's tail has a commit older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`. In that case the fork folds the source first, because the content that the fork takes from the tail is protected only while no commit in the tail is older than that limit (see the paragraph below on what keeps the content that the fork's manifest refers to). LoonFS checks the age of the tail that the fork folds, and not of an earlier view of the source that a process keeps (such as the position of a writer session on the source), because that view can lack commits that the tail the fork reads includes, and one of those commits can be the one that's too old.

Why does manifest 2 keep manifest 1's floor instead of the floor of the source's view? A floor object in the source's tail raises the view's floor, and a fold of the source would record the raised floor. A call that finishes a fork from manifest 1 reads the tail again only through the fork point, so it doesn't see a floor object after the tail's last commit. If manifest 2 applied floor objects, the manifest 2 that a finishing call publishes could have a lower floor than the one that the first call would have published. With manifest 1's floor, manifest 2's folded head, floor, and rows depend only on the tail's commits. The active manifest raises the floor to the fork point either way. A read through the fork point snapshot still finds every row between the source manifest's floor and the floor of the source's view, because the fork's manifests list the source manifest's segments.

Why a snapshot on the fork itself? Today the fork snapshot on the source keeps the source's manifest at the fork point, and three readers depend on that view: a client that reads a file as it was at the fork point, a rebase's conflict classification and its resolver, and grep's first step in a fork. When the source has an unfolded tail, it no longer publishes a manifest at the fork point, so the only manifest at the fork point is one that the fork publishes. The fork's own retention can't remove that manifest's history, because the fork point snapshot keeps the manifest as it was published, the same way the fork snapshot on the source keeps the source's manifest. The fork snapshot on the source stays, because the fork's manifests list the source's segments.

The fork point snapshot's write checks `METADATA_PUBLICATION_BUDGET_MS` first, as the fork snapshot's write does, so a call that started more than 15 minutes earlier writes nothing. Within the budget, a manifest that the call published is still in place, because a collection deletes a manifest of an initializing namespace only after a tombstone replaced it and the manifest is older than the grace window, and the grace window is longer than the budget. A call that finishes a fork can instead find an initializing manifest that's already older than the grace window (only a fork without a deadline can be that old and still initializing), and a delete and a collection can remove that manifest during the call, so the call can write the fork point snapshot on a deleted manifest. The deleted fork's collection keeps that snapshot for retirement without reporting corruption, because nothing reads through a deleted namespace's fork point snapshot and the tombstone lists the same runs.

Why is the fork made active by a separate manifest, and why is the floor raised last? Manifest 1 is the put-if-absent claim that decides which call creates the fork, and it records the fork basis that a finishing call needs to fold the tail again, so the fold writes its segments and extents only after manifest 1 exists, and the fold's result is a manifest of its own. The fork point snapshot has to exist before the fork is active. Otherwise a call that stopped right after activation would leave an active fork with no fork point snapshot, and no call would finish it. So the fork point snapshot is written on an initializing manifest, and the active manifest follows it. That initializing manifest keeps the source manifest's floor, so a read through the fork point snapshot finds the source's history at the fork point (its change feed, for example, and its revisions above that floor). If the source's tail has a floor object, the source manifest's floor is below the floor of the source's view, so the read finds more history than a read of the source would, and none is missing. The active manifest raises the floor to the fork point, because a fork's floor is its fork point ([Advancing the retention floor](../specs/format.md#101-advancing-the-retention-floor)). The fork's WAL has no commits before the namespace is active, so no floor object is needed to order the raise against a restore.

What keeps the source's tail while the fork folds it? The fork reads the tail's records in step 2, and the discovery checks that no fold replaced the source manifest while the tail was read, so the records are the source's history from the manifest's folded head through the fork point. A fold or a collection of the source after that read doesn't change what the fork writes. The temporary snapshot keeps the source manifest and its segments until the fork snapshot is verified, as today.

What keeps the content that the fork's manifest refers to? Under [Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots), every manifest that a member's snapshots keep is one of the member's layout views. The fork point snapshot keeps the fork's manifest at the fork point, so the content at the fork point stays for the fork's life, as it does today through the source's manifest. That includes the content of revisions that a later commit in the source's tail replaced, because the fork's run has the tail's whole history above the source's floor. The fork has no roots before its member entry is written. From then on, the manifest that its fork point snapshot keeps is one of its roots.

What protects the span objects that the fork's fold writes from a collection that missed the fork? A collection that listed the pool's members before the fork's member entry was written doesn't read the fork. Each span object that the fork's fold writes is new, so its age protects it for the grace window. But a reference that the fork takes from the tail also refers to the source's existing objects (an upload, or a span object that one of the source's earlier folds wrote), which can be older than the grace window, and only the source's roots protect those. They're protected because every reference that the fork takes from the tail was in the source's WAL tail when the fork read it, the fork takes the tail only while no commit in it is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`, and the fork becomes active within `OPERATION_SNAPSHOT_TTL_MS` (5 minutes) of that read or never becomes active. A retention advance of the source passes the tail's commits only after any collection that missed the fork has read the source (the inequality in [Publication and collection timing](../specs/format.md#c1-publication-and-collection-timing)), so that collection finds each reference in the source's current manifest or WAL tail, as the paragraph on a fork created during a collection in [Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots) describes.

Why is the deadline measured from the temporary snapshot's creation and not from its expiry? The inequality that the previous paragraph relies on reserves `OPERATION_SNAPSHOT_TTL_MS`, as one of its terms, for the time from the fork's read of the tail to its active manifest. The temporary snapshot is an ordinary user snapshot, and a caller can extend its expiry ([Extending a snapshot](../specs/format.md#84-extending-a-snapshot)), so the 5 minutes have to run from a time that the snapshot's record stores and that no extension changes. The snapshot's `created_at_ms` is the first call's start, which is no later than its read of the tail.

A call that finishes an initializing fork ([Finishing an initializing namespace](../specs/format.md#finishing-an-initializing-namespace)) continues from the newest manifest it finds. If manifest 1 is the newest and the fork point is above its folded head, the call folds the tail again, from the source's WAL objects above the source manifest's `folded_wal_no` through the object whose head is the fork point. The call reads those WAL objects whether or not the source has folded them since. The tail has a commit exactly when the fork point is above manifest 1's folded head, so a finishing call publishes a manifest 2 exactly when the first call would have, and that manifest 2 has the same folded head, floor, and rows as the one that the first call would have published (only the IDs of the segments and span objects that each call wrote differ). A finishing call doesn't check the tail's age again. It writes only before the fork's deadline, so the fork becomes active within 5 minutes of the first call's read of the tail or never becomes active, and those 5 minutes are one of the terms of the limit. If one of those WAL objects is gone, because a fold covered it and a collection deleted it, the call returns `snapshot_not_found`, which is the same error that a finishing call returns after the temporary snapshot expired, with the same outcome for the namespace. If two calls fold at once, the one whose manifest 2 put loses continues from the winner's manifest 2, and garbage collection deletes the loser's segments under the age rules. The loser wrote span objects of its own, which no layout lists, so garbage collection deletes them under the same rules.

### A merge writes the fork's pending pieces, as a copy does

[File contents and ownership](../specs/format.md#15-file-contents-and-ownership) and [Operations and WAL deltas](../specs/format.md#66-operations-and-wal-deltas) already say that before LoonFS admits a copy, a restore, or a new chain, validation writes the pending [pieces](../specs/glossary.md#piece) that the layout needs to content objects, the same way a fold does. A merge admits references from another member of the pool, so it's a copy across namespaces, and it does the same.

The merge plans from the fork's current manifest and WAL tail, which is the [read view](../specs/glossary.md#read-view) that every read of the fork's head uses. For each reference in the merge whose bytes are partly or wholly pieces in the fork's tail, the merge writes those pieces as the chain's next extent (see [Writing another member's pieces](#writing-another-members-pieces)), and includes the resulting layout in its commit. So every reference in a merge's commit has a layout, as today. A merge still includes no inline content and appends no bytes, and rule 1 of [WAL records](../specs/format.md#a5-wal-records) doesn't change. The planner admits each reference with the existing layout proof (`PreparedContent::for_pool_member`), whose layout comes from the fork's view and the extents that the merge just wrote. The fork's own later fold writes its own extent for the same pieces.

In the example, `sandbox` creates `/notes.txt` with 10 KiB at sequence 419, appends 2 KiB at 420, and merges before it folds. The merge writes the 12 KiB as a span object of `sandbox`'s chain, and the commit in `main` includes a layout with that extent, and the layout records `sandbox` as the chain's creator.

The merge still takes a snapshot of the fork while it plans, with the same 5-minute expiry, but on the fork's current manifest and without a fold. The snapshot protects a revision that the merge includes and that the fork replaced before the plan, which happens when the merge's range ends below the fork's head. The replacing commit can already be older than the grace window, so without the snapshot, a retention advance and a base compaction of the fork could remove the revision and its layout row right after the plan ([Content roots](../specs/format.md#119-content-roots)).

A snapshot keeps a manifest and not a WAL tail, so it doesn't cover a revision that the plan reads from the fork's tail. That case needs an old tail. Suppose the fork's tail includes a revision in the merge's range whose content has a layout (an upload, for example), and a later commit in the tail replaced it before the plan. If the replacing commit is older than the grace window less the other terms of the inequality in [Publication and collection timing](../specs/format.md#c1-publication-and-collection-timing), a fold, a retention advance, and a base compaction of the fork can remove the revision's layout row before a collection reads the fork, when that collection read main before the merge's commit. So a merge plans from the fork's WAL tail only while no commit in the fork's tail is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`:

```text
GC_GRACE_WINDOW_MS - (POOL_ROOTS_READ_BUDGET_MS + GC_SAFETY_MARGIN_MS
    + OPERATION_SNAPSHOT_TTL_MS + PROVIDER_PUBLICATION_REQUEST_BOUND_MS)
```

With the current values, the limit is 11 hours and 45 minutes. If the fork's tail has an older commit, the merge folds the fork first, as today, and plans from the manifest that the fold published. The reference implementation folds a tail once its oldest commit is 15 minutes old, the [fold age](../specs/glossary.md#fold-age) ([Recovery material and maintenance policy](../specs/format.md#73-recovery-material-and-maintenance-policy)), so a tail is normally far younger than the limit, and the fold is a backstop that rarely runs.

A retry of a merge with the same commit ID and merge record is a replay, because a merge's fingerprint covers its merge record in place of its operations ([Commit identity and retries](../specs/format.md#65-commit-identity-and-retries)). A retry whose plan writes the same pieces again writes new span objects, and garbage collection deletes the objects that no layout lists under the age rules. A rebase's replay commit is a merge into the rebase's new fork, so it takes the same path.

### Writing another member's pieces

A fork that folds its source's tail, and a merge that admits the fork's references, both write the pending pieces of a chain that another member of the pool created. One rule covers both.

When a namespace writes the pending pieces of a chain that another member created, the layout that it writes records that member as the [chain's creator](../specs/glossary.md#chain-creator), and it writes the pieces as one extent, which is never combined with the chain's existing extents. The creator comes from the chain's existing layout, or, for a chain that started in the tail, from rule 1 of [WAL records](../specs/format.md#a5-wal-records) applied to the WAL object that started the chain. That WAL object belongs to the creator, because a namespace's WAL includes pieces only for chains that the namespace created, so the creator is exact. The definition of a chain's creator, and the rule that only the creator extends a chain in place ([File contents and ownership](../specs/format.md#15-file-contents-and-ownership)), don't change.

A fold and validation's write of pending pieces share one plan, which applies the doubling rule to the chain's own extents (its earlier extents under its own content ID), and, when a fold runs in the chain's creator, to the shared base span extents that take the whole of their objects. A namespace that isn't the chain's creator doesn't combine any of the chain's extents with the pieces. So it writes only the pieces, and it never copies the creator's earlier bytes into an object that only its own layout lists.

Two writers of the same pieces don't share a key. A span object's key is the chain's content ID followed by a random span object ID that each write generates ([File contents and ownership](../specs/format.md#15-file-contents-and-ownership)), so each writer stores its own object, and each layout lists the object that its own write stored. Only the creator extends a chain, so both objects store the same bytes of the same chain.

For example, `main` started the chain of `/build.log` in commit 410. `sandbox`'s fold of `main`'s tail writes the chain's first 4 KiB to a span object of its own, and `main`'s own fold later writes the same 4 KiB to another span object, so the pool stores those 4 KiB twice, once for each namespace's layout.

Why does each writer store its own span object, instead of both writers writing one key built from the range? With one key, LoonFS would depend on an occupied key holding the same bytes, and that would be the only place where two namespaces write one key by design. With a random ID, a span object follows the rules of a metadata segment: one write creates it, a row that lists it keeps it, and garbage collection deletes it once no row lists it and it's old enough. The cost is that two writers of the same pieces store them twice.

### What stays the same

A content ID never changes. A reference identifies the same object in every member of a pool, and a merge compares the fork's rows with main's rows by reference, so if a fork or a merge assigned content new IDs, inherited revisions would look like changes. Nothing in this design copies content. The only bytes it writes are pieces written to extents, which is what a fold does.

The design adds one snapshot for each fork, on the fork itself. It adds no new kind of root and no copy of a WAL object. The fork point snapshot is a fork snapshot, and the manifest that it keeps is a layout view under the existing rule for the manifests that a member's snapshots keep.

Two properties hold everywhere, as they do today. The pieces in a namespace's WAL belong to chains that the namespace created, so replay's inference of a tail chain's creator stays exact. Every reference that a namespace admits from another member has a layout, so a merge's commit includes no bytes, and its size doesn't depend on how much of the fork is folded.

## Reading the fork point

A client reads a fork as it was at the fork point with `snapshot_id` set to the fork's fork point snapshot, which the namespace object's `fork_basis.fork_point_snapshot_id` identifies. The read is a read of the fork's own namespace, and it returns the source's state at the fork point, because the snapshot's manifest lists the source's runs and the fork's run of the source's tail, with the source manifest's floor. The fork snapshot on the source is no longer part of the API, because nothing reads the source through it, and only the fork's retirement uses its ID.

A rebase reads main at the new fork's fork point through the new fork's fork point snapshot, both for its conflict classification (main's commit rows, the base version, and the subtree diff up to that point) and for the resolver's read of each conflict's base. The snapshot's manifest has the floor of main's manifest. That floor is at or below the floor of main's head, and the rebase checks the replayed fork's fork point against the head's floor before it creates the new fork. So the base revision and main's history between the two fork points are in the snapshot's manifest.

Grep's first step in a fork reads the gap between the inherited index's watermark and the fork point through the fork point snapshot. The snapshot's manifest lists the source's runs and the fork's run, so one view has every commit from the source manifest's floor through the fork point, and the step reads nothing from the source.

## Costs

This section lists what the design costs, compared with folding the other namespace first.

- A fork of a head whose tail has a commit makes three more requests than today. The fold's metadata segments move from the source's prefix to the fork's (its content writes stay under the pool's prefix), and the source's manifest put becomes the fork's manifest 2 put. The fork adds the fork point snapshot's put and one more manifest put with its boundary read. A fork of a user snapshot, or of a head whose tail has no commit, adds the fork point snapshot's put. The check of the tail's age reads the view that the fork reads, so it sends no request of its own. (Today, runtime tests expect 15 requests for a fork of a folded source in a runtime whose writer session on the source has a fresh position, and 22 in a runtime with nothing cached. With this design, the fork with nothing cached sends 23, and a fork with a fresh position of a source whose tail has one commit sends 18 requests, plus the fold's one content write and its 7 metadata segments.)
- The source's tail is folded twice over time, once by the fork when it's created and once by the source on its own schedule. So one tail's metadata segments are stored twice, and its pieces are written to span objects twice. Neither duplicate is larger than one tail. A source that's forked several times between two of its own folds has its tail folded once more by each fork, and each fork pays for its own fold, because no fork folds the source. While the source's writer runs, it bounds the tail by its fold trigger, so one fork pays for at most one trigger's worth of commits. Measured against S3, 32 forks of a namespace of 10,000 files that had 4 commits and no fold between them each took about twice as long as a fork of the same namespace after a fold (944 ms against 473 ms at the median).
- A delete folds the namespace's tail before it publishes the tombstone ([Deleting a namespace](../specs/format.md#94-deleting-a-namespace)). Today a fork that's merged and then deleted is folded by the merge, so the delete finds nothing to fold. With this design, the delete folds the fork's tail. The work is the same, in a different call. Measured against S3, the delete of a fork right after its merge went from 2 requests to 82.
- For a fork, the gain is ownership, not latency. While no commit in the source's tail is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`, the source publishes nothing for a fork, and its fold schedule is its own.
- A merge writes one extent for each chain with pending pieces in its range, and it doesn't write the fork's segments or publish a manifest of the fork. The fork's later fold writes the same pieces again, to a span object of its own, so the pool stores those pieces twice for as long as a layout lists each of the two span objects. Measured on an in-memory store with a release build, a merge's fold of a fork that has 1,000 unfolded files of 1 KiB writes 1,000 content objects, 7 metadata segments, and 1 manifest, and building the segments takes 51 ms of the fold's 81 ms. With 100 files, it writes 100 content objects and the same 7 metadata segments and 1 manifest. So the design saves those 8 metadata writes and most of the fold's processor time on every merge, and the content writes stay, because they are the fold's own work done earlier. The content writes go out 32 at a time, so against an object store a merge of 1,000 unfolded small files still waits for about 32 rounds of writes, and a merge of files that already have layouts waits for none.
- A merge whose fork has a tail commit older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS` (11 hours and 45 minutes) folds the fork first, as every merge does today, and a fork of a head whose tail has such a commit folds the source first, as every fork does today.
- Every fork keeps two fork snapshot records until its retirement, one on its source and one on itself, and its retirement deletes both.

## What the format and API specs change

In the storage format:

- [Manifest references](../specs/format.md#25-manifest-references) says that the fork basis includes the IDs of the fork's two fork snapshots.
- [Resolving the metadata basis](../specs/format.md#41-resolving-the-metadata-basis) says that a fork's `fork_basis` records its fork point and both fork snapshots.

## Alternatives considered

- **Folding the other namespace first.** This is the current design. Each fork of a head with an unfolded tail makes the source publish a manifest, and each merge makes the fork publish one. A fork's call races the source's writer on the source's fold.
- **Copying the source's WAL records into the fork's WAL.** The fork writes the source's tail records as its own first WAL objects, and reads them like any tail. Replay then has to accept WAL objects that record another namespace's ID and a writer epoch of 0, and the fork needs a floor object to reach its floor. The fork's manifests never describe the fork point, so keeping the fork point readable needs a retention exception in the fork or a new root for the copied objects. A namespace's WAL would also include pieces of chains that another member created.
- **Including the fork's pieces in the merge's commit.** The merge includes the fork's pending pieces as inline content, and main's fold writes them. Main's view would then take a chain's creator from the merge record, which identifies the wrong namespace after a rebase, unless the WAL format adds a creator field. A later merge of an appended chain needs a rule to include only the new pieces, the planner needs a proof that includes pieces under an existing content ID, and the 4 MiB limit on inline content in one WAL object splits merges of large unfolded forks. A namespace would also admit references from another member without a layout.
- **Reading the source's WAL objects in place.** The fork snapshot keeps a range of the source's WAL objects as well as its manifest, and the fork replays them from the source's prefix. The fork's view then has a tail from another namespace, the snapshot record lists WAL objects, and the source's collection has to keep WAL objects that its own manifest has folded.
- **Copying the tail's content under new IDs.** The fork writes the tail's pieces under content IDs that it creates. Every inherited revision then has a reference that differs from main's, so the next merge applies inherited revisions as changes or reports them as conflicts.
- **Asking the source's writer to fold at the fork point.** The source still folds, the fork depends on the source's writer being reachable, and a fold covers the tail that it finds, so it publishes a manifest at the fork point only if no commit is added in between.
- **A new snapshot owner for the fork point snapshot.** The snapshot keeps a manifest until the fork's retirement, like a fork snapshot, and readers and collections already handle fork snapshots, so a new owner would add a third set of rules for the same lifetime.
