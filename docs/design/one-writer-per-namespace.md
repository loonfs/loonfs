# One writer per namespace

This note describes which process writes a namespace's WAL and publishes its manifests. One process at a time does both, the namespace's "writer", whose [writer session](../specs/glossary.md#writer-session) has the namespace's writer epoch, and it sends its WAL puts through one serial queue and its manifest publications through another. Other processes read the namespace, write their own records beside it, collect it, and can build compaction output and index segments for the writer to install.

## The problem

A namespace's WAL and manifests are written by processes that don't coordinate. The writer session commits, any process can fold or compact the namespace and publish a manifest as its "maintainer" (a process that publishes the namespace's folds and compaction results without its writer epoch), and any process can write a [floor object](../specs/glossary.md#floor-object) to the WAL to advance the retention floor. Inside one process, a session's commits, its folds and its compaction units publish beside one another. Each is ordered by put-if-absent at the next number alone, so each is a race that needs rules for its loser. Two consequences have been measured, and a third was constructed in a test from the code:

- A retention advance from a process without the writer session wins the next WAL number about 5% of the time against a saturated writer of small commits, so an advance takes 19 attempts and about 13 seconds at p50, and the writer pays one refused put and one discovery per advance (lab record `discovery-fix-on-s3-20261126`).
- Folds and compaction units race for each manifest number, inside one process and from another host. At four publications a second in one process, a fold lost 0.86 puts at the next manifest number for each manifest it published, and 22 of 2,459 folds lost every attempt and returned `store_contention` (lab record `small-publication-rate-20261126`). Against a writer whose process published about 4.7 manifests a second, 3 of 100 snapshots from another host failed, one because the fold before it lost that race and two because a discovery found a newer manifest 8 times (`discovery-fix-on-s3-20261126`).
- A late floor object can leave a namespace unreadable. This case hasn't been observed in a run, and it needs two pauses longer than the 24-hour grace window. Suppose a new writer publishes claim manifest 8 over manifest 7 (`folded_wal_no` 30, head 140), and the old writer commits WAL object 31 (sequence 141) before the new writer's fence. A maintainer folds 31 into manifest 9, and a collection deletes 31 a day later. If a process that planned a floor object against manifest 7 pauses for more than a day before its put, the floor object is written at the empty number 31 with head 140. If the new writer also pauses for more than a day between its claim and its fence's discovery, the discovery replays the floor object, and the writer accepts a fence at 32 with head 140 because 32 is above manifest 9's `folded_wal_no`. Readers of manifest 9 then find a fence whose head is below the manifest's and report the namespace corrupt. The case exists because the check after a fence's put compares the fence's WAL number with the newest manifest's `folded_wal_no` and doesn't compare the head that the fence records, which came from a discovery that replayed a dead floor object.

## The rule

A namespace has one writer at a time: the process that has claimed its [writer epoch](../specs/glossary.md#writer-epoch), by publishing a claim manifest and writing a [fence](../specs/glossary.md#fence) (see [Writer ownership](../specs/format.md#61-writer-ownership)). Only the namespace's writer writes its WAL and publishes its manifests. That covers every commit, fold, compaction result, extension entry (such as grep's), retention window change, retention floor advance, merge and namespace deletion. The rule applies to an active namespace, and creating the namespace is what makes it active.

Every manifest publication of an active namespace other than a claim is a lane item that takes the writer session's position as its basis, puts once at the next manifest number, and on success moves the position onto the manifest that it published. The one function that publishes manifests requires a position for these publications, so none can skip the rule. A session gets its position from its claim's discovery or from the writer's load, which compares the epoch of each manifest that the load finds with the session's epoch. A higher epoch is another writer's claim and returns `writer_fenced`, and a lower one returns `namespace_corrupt`. That load is the only place where epochs are compared before a manifest put. A claim builds on the newest manifest instead, and builds again on each manifest that wins its number.

The same function checks the segments that a publication adds and when their writing started, as [The requirement on publications](replaced-manifest-window.md#the-requirement-on-publications) describes. Beside that check, every publication keeps the publication budget (`METADATA_PUBLICATION_BUDGET_MS`, 15 minutes from the start of the publication to its put), which is the bound on a fold. Install passes a build result's outputs through the same check, with their oldest provider timestamp in place of a start time (see [Building outside the writer](#building-outside-the-writer)). Before the put, the function also validates the candidate as a reader's decoding does, so no publication can put a manifest that readers refuse, such as one that lists a segment in two runs or two base runs in one family group.

An initializing namespace has no WAL and no writer, and the format allows three manifests after an initializing one: a fork's manifest 2, the activation, and the tombstone (see [Publishing a manifest](../specs/format.md#72-publishing-a-manifest)). So four callers race for that manifest with put-if-absent: the creator, a caller in any process that finishes an interrupted creation, a deletion of the initializing namespace, and a collection that tombstones an abandoned one. A caller whose put loses acts on the manifest that won, so an activation or a tombstone that loses to a fork's manifest 2 builds on that manifest. The first claim after activation makes the first writer.

## The publication lane

The writer has two serial queues, each of which sends one put at a time. The "commit queue" is the writer session's queue, which sends its WAL puts. The "publication lane" is the second queue, for every manifest publication of the writer: a fold's publication, an install, an extension entry, a retention window change, a retention floor advance, a deletion's manifests, and a snapshot's record put with its verification (so no publication of the writer replaces the snapshot's manifest in between). Manifest publications never race each other, and a WAL put and a manifest put can be in flight at the same time, so a commit doesn't wait for a manifest item.

A lane item is a change that the writer applies to its own position's manifest when the item's turn comes. The session has that manifest in memory, so an item reads nothing from the store before its put. The heavy work happens before the item enters the lane: a fold writes its content objects and segments, a compaction unit rewrites its runs, and a grep step writes its segments. A change whose work another item has already covered publishes nothing (for example, an install whose inputs another install replaced), and collection deletes its segments by age.

A retention floor advance and a deletion pause commits for their last step, and only for it. Each keeps the lane and pauses the commit queue for a fold of what arrived during an ordinary fold, so the manifest that each publishes covers every commit written before it, and every commit after it sees the new floor or the tombstone (see [The retention floor](#the-retention-floor)).

Because the writer publishes one manifest at a time and no other process publishes, a manifest put that the writer loses means another claim. The put either collides with the claim's manifest or, if a collection has already deleted that manifest, is written at its number, and the boundary read after the put reports the publication as lost. Both return `writer_fenced`, and the item doesn't build on the winner or put again. A WAL put that collides with the claim's fence returns `writer_fenced` too, and either way the session's other queue stops. A missing segment of the position's manifest means a claim or a damaged store, and one read for a newer manifest tells them apart.

## The epoch around every put

Under [Failed and unknown outcomes](../specs/format.md#64-failed-and-unknown-outcomes), a session releases its epoch when it sends a commit's WAL put or a tombstone's put and takes it back when it learns the outcome, so after an unknown outcome, or a task that ends while its put is in flight, the next publication claims a new epoch and writes a fence before it plans anything. This design applies the rule to every manifest put, across both queues, and states it by outcomes, because a release at each send would leave a commit without an epoch while a fold's put is in flight. The session has its epoch from its claim until any put of either queue ends with an unknown outcome, and a put whose task ends before it learns the outcome counts as one. Each queue starts a unit only while the session has its epoch, and a unit that has started sends its put, even when the session loses its epoch before the send, because the next claim waits for that put's outcome and its discovery reads what the put wrote. A put already in flight in the other queue is settled when its own outcome arrives, but a known outcome never restores the epoch after an unknown one. If it did, the session would publish again without a claim, and its next manifest could collide with its own late one. The next publication claims once neither queue has a put in flight, and the claim keeps the lane and the commit queue while it runs, as a tombstone does, because it publishes a manifest and puts a fence.

A lane item whose put ends with an unknown outcome ends there and returns the unknown outcome to its caller, as a commit does. A manifest item learns its outcome only when both its put and the boundary read after the put have answered, so a successful put followed by a failed boundary read is an unknown outcome too, and the next publication claims. An unknown outcome drops the session's position together with its epoch, so nothing in the process plans or reads from that position until the claim's discovery builds a new one. (A kept position could be one manifest behind a written put, so a deletion would fold WAL objects again, and a read would leave out a written commit.) A session that still has its epoch but no position (the position was dropped, or its manifest is gone) rebuilds its position with the writer's load, without a claim.

Every other manifest put needs the rule for the reason that the format states for a tombstone: a put whose outcome the writer didn't learn can still be written later, at its number. The writer's next publication is then a claim at that number, which either collides with the late manifest or takes the number so that the late manifest collides. The same order keeps a lost manifest put meaning another claim.

## The retention floor

The retention floor is the manifest's `retention_floor_seq`, and the writer raises it through its lane. To advance the floor, the writer:

1. Plans the target from its position's manifest under the rules in [Advancing the retention floor](../specs/format.md#101-advancing-the-retention-floor): the three targets, the cutoff walk over the `commits` family, and the grace-window bound. If the target isn't above the floor, it writes nothing.
2. Checks that every segment of that manifest exists.
3. Takes the namespace's fold lock, which keeps its folds one at a time, so it waits for a running fold while commits continue.
4. Folds the tail as an ordinary fold, whose publication is an ordinary lane item.
5. Still under the fold lock, keeps the lane and pauses the commit queue, folds what arrived during step 4 if anything did, and publishes a manifest that includes that fold and whose floor is the higher of the old floor and the target. It publishes this manifest even when nothing arrived, because the floor is in it. It then releases the commit queue, the lane and the lock. While the commit queue is paused it sends no new WAL put, and the fold starts only after the WAL put already in flight is settled. Otherwise a restore validated against the old floor could be written above the manifest that raised the floor.

A deletion takes steps 3 to 5. In step 5, once it keeps the lane and has paused the commit queue, it waits for the WAL put in flight to be settled and checks `expected_head_seq` against its position's head, which is then exact because no other commit is written while the queue is paused, so a deletion whose head moved fails before it folds or publishes in step 5. It then folds what arrived and publishes that fold's manifest (or none, if nothing arrived), and then publishes the tombstone as its own manifest, whose `folded_wal_no` is the position's tip, while both queues are still kept, because a commit acknowledged while the tombstone is in flight would be hidden by it. If the fold of step 4 fails, the advance or the deletion returns the fold's error, and the floor and the namespace's status haven't changed. If another put of the session ends with an unknown outcome while the operation is under way, the operation continues. Before step 5, whichever publication comes next claims, and the operation continues from the new position. In step 5 the operation already keeps both queues, so the operation itself claims, then folds what arrived from the claim's position and publishes, with the target or the expected head it started with. A deletion checks its expected head after the claim, so a commit that the claim's discovery found makes the deletion fail as a moved head does. An operation whose own put ends with an unknown outcome, including the put of the fold of step 4, ends there and returns that outcome.

Every fold in the writer's process runs under the fold lock, and the order is always the fold lock, then the lane, then the commit queue. Only the fold lock before the lane is needed to avoid a deadlock, and the commit queue comes last so that commits pause as late as possible. At its turn, a fold's item publishes when the position's manifest still has the `folded_wal_no` that the fold started from, and publishes nothing when that manifest already covers the fold's tail. No manifest can cover only part of the fold's range, because folds run one at a time under the fold lock and each starts from the session's position. An ordinary fold has the fold lock while its publication waits for its turn in the lane, so an operation that kept the lane and then waited for the fold lock would stop both. Because the advance and the deletion keep the lock across both folds, no other fold of the namespace starts in between.

Commits pause for the fold of step 5, once per advance (once a day for each namespace under the sweep) and once per deletion. That fold covers what arrived during one ordinary fold, so commits wait at most about as long as an ordinary fold takes. In `floor-advance-contention-20261126`, a fold of a few commits took 156 ms at p50. With 60 KiB commits, a fold took 0.45 s at p50, and by estimate the writer writes 8 MiB, a fold threshold, in about that time, so the fold of step 5 is an ordinary fold's size. With 300-byte commits, a fold of up to 256 WAL objects took 2.7 s at p50 (3.9 s at p90), and the writer writes about 80 WAL objects in that time (one every 34 ms), so by estimate the fold of step 5 is about a third of that size. A tail can't pass the write stop (1,024 objects, 64 MiB, or 32 MiB of inline bytes), and by estimate a fold of a tail at the stop takes 10 to 16 seconds, inside the 30-second write-stop wait, so even then commits are delayed but not refused.

There is no floor object. A view's floor is its manifest's floor, and readers read it there. A restore written before step 5 pauses the commit queue is folded in step 4 or step 5, so its `revoke` event is in the manifest that records the new floor. A restore queued behind step 5 is validated at its turn in the commit queue against the new floor. The writer validates every restore against its own position's floor, which only its lane changes, so nothing has to be ordered against another process. If the writer stops before the manifest is written, the floor hasn't moved, and the next advance moves it.

The expired-deletion invariant then has a short argument. Take a manifest `M` whose floor the advance raised. Every WAL object at or below `M`'s `folded_wal_no` is folded into `M`. Every WAL object above it was written after the advance released the commit queue, by this writer against the new floor, or by a later writer whose claim built on `M` or a later manifest. A put sent before the advance whose outcome the writer didn't learn can't be written above `M`'s fold, by the argument in [Publication and group commit](../specs/format.md#63-publication-and-group-commit). Only the advance's step 5 and a fork's active manifest raise a manifest's floor, and every other publication copies it.

## The fold age

A session's fold is due once the tail reaches a fold threshold, or once the oldest unfolded commit is older than the fold age (15 minutes by default). So a writer that commits every 10 minutes still folds about every 15 minutes. A host with a scheduler, such as the reference server, checks its sessions' fold age at every maintenance interval, so a live writer whose folds succeed never has a tail much older than the fold age. An embedded session checks the fold age only when it publishes, so one that goes quiet keeps its unfolded tail, and a fork or a merge from another process that needs the tail folded claims the namespace (the embedded writer's next commit claims it back). The fold age always applies and can't be turned off, and it has to be below `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS` (11 hours and 45 minutes).

## Everyone else

Other processes read the namespace and create side records under their own keys with put-if-absent: user snapshots, fork snapshots and temporary snapshots, member entries at creation, and upload sessions and their outcome records. A snapshot's deletion and extension stay a compare-and-swap on its record.

Collection is unchanged. A namespace's collection reads the newest manifest and the snapshot listing, deletes what they don't reference once the age rules allow, raises the [manifest boundary](../specs/glossary.md#manifest-boundary) before it deletes manifests, writes a tombstone for an abandoned initializing namespace, and at retirement deletes the fork snapshots and the member entry. A pool's collection deletes content and upload records and writes the aborted outcome of an expired upload session. None of these is a WAL object or a manifest of an active namespace, so collection needs no writer.

## Changing a namespace that another process writes

A process that needs a namespace's WAL or manifest changed claims the namespace first. A server does that when a write or a maintenance call arrives for a namespace that it isn't the writer of. A fork from the current head and a merge fold the source or the fork first when a commit in its tail is older than `MAX_TAIL_COMMIT_AGE_MS`, so they claim it for that fold.

Because of the fold age, a tail that old belongs to a namespace that nobody has claimed since its last commit, or to a writer whose folds have kept failing for hours. A fork's or a merge's claim displaces a live writer only in that second case.

A snapshot's record is a side record, so a snapshot of the current head needs a fold, and so a claim when its process isn't the writer, only when the view has a commit above its manifest's head. (A tail of only fences counts as empty, as it does for a fork's fold of its source's tail.) Otherwise the snapshot writes its record on the current manifest and verifies it with the successor probe and the boundary read, as [Creating and verifying a snapshot](../specs/format.md#82-creating-and-verifying-a-snapshot) describes. If the probe finds a successor, the snapshot deletes its candidate and tries again from a fresh basis, up to 8 attempts (`MAX_CONTENTION_ATTEMPTS`), after which it returns `store_contention`. An attempt that finds a commit in its tail claims, and a snapshot that runs as the writer isn't refused, as [The publication lane](#the-publication-lane) describes. A control plane that snapshots idle namespaces then leaves no fence, claim manifest or higher epoch on them.

## The reply and one retry

A request that finds a newer writer gets `writer_fenced`, whose details include the fenced epoch, the newer epoch, and the newer writer's writer block: its writer ID and when it claimed. The server's writer ID is unique to the process, because the server adds an ID that it generates at start to the configured `writer_id`.

When a session finds that it's fenced, it cancels its compaction unit, so its drain ends once the requests in flight and any running fold finish. The server then retries the fenced request once in a new session, whose first publication claims again. A request is safe to send again when the fenced attempt wrote nothing that the retry could repeat, and the retry checks the request's identity or preconditions against the new state. That's true of every request that can be fenced:

- **A commit, a merge and a replay.** `writer_fenced` comes from a discovery before the put or after a collision, so the fenced attempt's put wasn't written (a claim that finds a newer epoch returns `writer_fenced` before the commit's put). The retry's receipt check, with the same `commit_id` and fingerprint, replays an earlier put of the request that committed. A retry that is fenced before its receipt check hasn't looked for an earlier put whose outcome was unknown, which may have committed or may still commit. The retry of a merge or a replay merges the range that the first attempt resolved, as a merge that plans again after a stale plan does, so a fork commit written in between isn't merged. Preconditions are evaluated again.
- **A namespace deletion.** The tombstone is its last put, so a fenced deletion wrote none, and the retry checks `expected_head_seq` again while the commit queue is paused.
- **A maintenance job and an install.** Each plans from the current state, so a repeat publishes at most what is due, and a repeated install is refused once its inputs are gone.
- **A snapshot and a fork.** One that claims is fenced during its fold, before it writes its snapshot record or the fork's manifest 1. A snapshot whose verification finds a claim marks its candidate deleted first, as a failed creation does. A snapshot with nothing to fold doesn't claim, so it isn't fenced, and a successor at its verification means it tries again.

Reads and uploads never get `writer_fenced`. If the retry is displaced too, the client gets the reply, which identifies the newer writer, and a metric records each displacement. When a put of the request had an unknown outcome, the server returns `outcome_unknown` instead, as the publisher does for any failure after an unknown outcome, because that put can still commit.

The core has no other way to choose which process writes a namespace: no timer, liveness check, delay, lease, or directory of writers. Placement is up to whatever sends requests to servers.

## What a deployment uses

A control plane uses four operations:

- **Claim.** A server claims a namespace on first use, when a request first needs to change it. There is no claim route.
- **Release.** A route that closes the namespace's session for a planned move. The close rejects new work with `writer_session_closed`, drains the admitted commits, cancels the compaction unit, and waits for a running fold to finish (a fold that succeeds publishes its manifest). The close adds no put of its own and doesn't fold. The next writer pays one claim (2 puts and about 6 + k reads for a tail of k WAL objects, about 200 ms), reads the unfolded tail once, and starts with cold caches.
- **Maintain.** The maintenance jobs (`metadata`, `retention`, `retention_window` and `install`) and the routes that enable and disable the grep index run as the writer on the server that receives them, which claims first when it isn't the writer.
- **The reply.** `writer_fenced` after the one retry, or `outcome_unknown` when a put of the request had an unknown outcome, as above.

To see what needs work, a control plane uses the reads that exist. The namespace diagnostics route reports the head, the floor, the manifest number, the number of unfolded WAL objects, and the time of the oldest unfolded commit in Unix milliseconds (`wal_tail_oldest_commit_at_ms`), which is absent when the tail has no commit (a tail of only fences has none). So a control plane reads whether a fold is due by age instead of sending a `metadata` job, and it can see the unfolded tail of an embedded session that went quiet without displacing anyone. The manifest records the retention window, and a worker's planner reports whether a compaction unit is due in the manifest that it read. A `metadata` job runs as the writer and so claims, which makes it an action, not a read.

A single server that maintains its whole store needs no control plane. Its sweep checks each namespace with the reads that show whether a fold, a compaction unit or an advance is due, and claims the ones with work due.

## Building outside the writer

A "build" is the work of a compaction unit or a grep step, and an "install" is its one publication. A control plane can run builds in "workers", processes other than the writer, and chooses where and when they run.

A server setting, `writer_builds` (default `true`), controls whether the writer builds its own compaction output and index segments. With `writer_builds = false`, workers build and the writer only installs. Folding stays with the writer either way, because the writer folds from its session's position without reading the WAL, and the fold's publication has its place in the lane against the floor advance and the deletion. If the setting is off and no worker hands anything back, runs accumulate and the planner reports a compaction unit as due. The writer doesn't start building after a delay.

A build needs no claim. A worker reads the namespace as any reader does, plans a unit from the manifest that it read with the planner the writer uses, writes the output segments with fresh IDs as a compaction unit does, and publishes nothing. Its "build result" lists:

| Field | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| `kind` | `metadata_compaction` or `extension` |
| `planned_from` | The [manifest reference](../specs/format.md#25-manifest-references) of the manifest that the build read, whose head a base output is placed at |
| Inputs | For `metadata_compaction`, the family group and the input run numbers with their segments. For `extension`, the extension's name and the entry that the build started from. |
| Outputs | For `metadata_compaction`, each output's segment ID and what its descriptor records about the segment's contents. For `extension`, the new entry. |

The result records no time, because the writer doesn't use a worker's clock, and no floor, because a base compacted at one floor stays valid at every higher floor. The writer writes each output's descriptor with its own namespace as the owner (`owner_namespace_id`), so a result can't list another namespace's segment as an output.

Install is a writer operation that takes one build result, and the server offers it as the `install` kind of the maintenance job route. The writer's compaction calls the same install with a result it built itself, so there is one path. That result takes the same two checks, and its outputs get the same HEADs and the same timestamp check, which costs one HEAD per output for each compaction unit, sent before the item enters the lane. The writer checks the result against its position's manifest:

1. **Inputs.** The manifest still lists the input runs with the same segments, or, for an extension, has the entry that the build started from. An install whose inputs are gone is refused, whatever replaced them, including an earlier request that installed this same result.
2. **Outputs.** Each output exists with the stored checksum that its descriptor records (one HEAD each, sent before the item enters the lane). A missing output is refused, because it has no timestamp to check and a collection may already have deleted it.

Then the writer publishes the replacement manifest through its lane. The output is a base run at `planned_from`'s `head_seq` when the unit's window includes the group's oldest run, and otherwise a delta run at its newest input's `run_seq`, and its `run_no` is the installing manifest's `next_run_no`. Install passes the publication function the outputs and the oldest provider timestamp that the HEADs returned, the timestamp from which a collection computes an unlisted segment's age. Immediately before the put, the function refuses the put when that timestamp is more than 11 hours and 55 minutes before the writer's clock (`METADATA_COMPACTION_BUDGET_MS` minus `GC_SAFETY_MARGIN_MS`), and it refuses a candidate that readers would refuse (see [The rule](#the-rule)). Nothing references a refused result's outputs, so collection deletes them by age. `installed` is the answer of the request that published.

Why is the age bound enough? A collection deletes an unlisted segment once its provider age is 24 hours (`GC_GRACE_WINDOW_MS`). When the manifest lists the output, a collector finds it at most 12 hours and 5 minutes old: the 11 hours and 55 minutes of the check, plus `PROVIDER_PUBLICATION_REQUEST_BOUND_MS` (5 minutes, within which the provider applies the put), plus `GC_SAFETY_MARGIN_MS` (5 minutes, the most that the writer's and the collector's clocks differ, see [Clock and operation assumptions](../specs/format.md#114-clock-and-operation-assumptions)). That is below the 24-hour grace window.

What install does in each case:

| Case | Install |
| --- | --- |
| Two workers build the same unit, or the writer compacts the same inputs | The first to reach its turn publishes, and the other is refused. |
| A result built from a manifest that the writer has since replaced | Installs if the inputs are still listed. |
| A retried install | Refused once its inputs are gone, including after its own success. The caller plans again from the current manifest, and nothing is lost. |
| The writer changes between build and install | The result doesn't refer to a writer, so the new writer installs it under the same checks. |
| An empty output (a base whose rows the floor drops) | The manifest drops the inputs and adds nothing. |
| A faulty worker | It can't make a manifest list a segment that doesn't exist, that a collection can delete, or that readers refuse. Install doesn't read rows, so a worker is trusted as any process with write access to the store is, and install is in the `admin` scope. Its segments can drop, add or change any row of the family group that it compacted (a dropped commit receipt lets a retried commit apply twice, and a dropped tombstone row brings a deleted file back). |

The search index fits. A grep step already ends with a publication that lists the entry it started from and the new entry, and the publication refuses a change whose starting entry was replaced. That is install's input check, so grep steps run in a worker hand back the same way.

## A day of one namespace

The namespace `docs` starts the day with manifest 40 (writer epoch 3, `folded_wal_no` 300, head 5,000, floor 4,000, retention window 7 days), and no process has claimed it since its last fold. Two of its deletions are 8 days old: `/old` at sequence 4,500 and `/tmp` at sequence 4,600. Server A has writer ID `a-7k2m` and server B `b-9q4t`.

1. **09:00, first commit.** A receives a commit and opens a session. Its first publication claims: manifest 41 with epoch 4 and A's writer block, a discovery that finds WAL 301 empty, and a fence at 301 with head 5,000. The commit is written at WAL 302 as sequence 5,001.
2. **09:15, a fold.** The oldest unfolded commit, at 302, reaches the 15-minute fold age with the tail at 420 (sequence 5,119). The fold writes its segments while commits continue at 421 to 424, and its lane item puts manifest 42 (`folded_wal_no` 420, head 5,119) and reads the boundary while the commit at 425 is written. By 14:00, such folds and compaction units have published through manifest 60 (`folded_wal_no` 690).
3. **14:00, the floor passes a deletion.** The control plane sends `retention` to A. The cutoff walk from floor 4,000 stops at sequence 4,800, the last commit more than 7 days old. The advance's ordinary fold folds 691 to 698 into manifest 61 while commits continue, and a restore of `/old` is written meanwhile at 701 (sequence 6,301). Then the advance keeps the lane and pauses the commit queue, folds 699 to 701, and publishes manifest 62 (`folded_wal_no` 701, head 6,301, floor 4,800). A restore of `/tmp` queued behind it is validated against floor 4,800, and its deletion at 4,600 has expired, so it fails (see [Expiring deletions and creating floor objects](../specs/format.md#expiring-deletions-and-creating-floor-objects) for the error). `/old` stays restored, because its `revoke` event is in manifest 62.
4. **14:10, a second server.** A misrouted commit reaches B, which claims manifest 63 with epoch 5, finds A's last commit at WAL 710, writes its fence at 711, and commits at 712. A's next commit collides with B's fence, finds epoch 5, and is fenced. A cancels the session's compaction, opens a new session, claims manifest 64 with epoch 6, fences at 713, and commits the request at 714 after its receipt check finds no earlier commit. The commit is about 200 ms slower, and A's displacement metric rises.
5. **17:00, deletion.** A's folds and compaction units have published through manifest 70 (`folded_wal_no` 900) when a client that read the head at WAL 930 sends the namespace's deletion with that head as `expected_head_seq`. A takes the fold lock and folds WAL 901 to 930 into manifest 71, and no commit arrives meanwhile. Then it keeps the lane and pauses the commit queue, checks that the head is still the expected one, finds nothing more to fold, and publishes the tombstone, manifest 72, with epoch 6. B's fenced session finds the namespace deleted on its next open.

## What happens to the three races

- **A retention advance against a busy writer.** The advance is an item in the writer's lane, so it collides with nothing.
- **Folds and compaction units racing for manifest numbers.** They take turns in the writer's lane, and a snapshot from another host folds only as the writer, so no fold loses a number. A discovery from another host still meets the writer's manifests, so it can still find a newer manifest 8 times. Against 4.7 manifests a second, no lookup, listing or fork failed in 600 tries, and 2 of 100 snapshots failed that way (`discovery-fix-on-s3-20261126`).
- **A late marker.** There is no floor object, and between a claim and its fence no other process can publish a manifest (the old writer's fold is written before the claim or is fenced), so no fold covers a WAL number between the claim and the fence.

## What each caller does

| Caller | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| A write | Goes to the writer's server. A server that isn't the writer claims, and a displaced request is retried once. |
| A read | Reads in any process. In the writer's process it reads from the session's position. |
| A maintenance job | Runs as the writer, claiming first when needed. `gc` runs anywhere. |
| A fork | Writes the new namespace's initializing manifests and the snapshots on the source. Claims the source only to fold a tail with a commit older than 11 hours and 45 minutes. |
| A merge, a rebase, a replay | Commits to the target as its writer, and claims the fork only to fold an old tail, as a fork does. |
| A snapshot | Of the current head with a commit in its tail, folds as the writer (its process claims first when it isn't the writer), and writes and verifies its record in the lane. Otherwise, and for deletion and extension, writes the record from any process. |
| A collection | Runs anywhere, unchanged. |
| The CLI | With a remote profile, sends requests to a server. With an embedded profile, its runtime claims for writes and for maintenance, which displaces a running server. |
| The embedded library | Its runtime is a process like any other: writes and maintenance claim, reads don't. Maintenance that publishes is a method of the writable namespace, not of `Maintenance`. The library returns `writer_fenced` without a retry. |

## Costs

- The writer's process does its own folds, and its own compaction and index builds unless `writer_builds` is off.
- Commits pause for a fold of what arrived during one ordinary fold, at each floor advance and each deletion.
- A writer that commits less often than the fold age folds once per commit, and one that commits more often folds whenever its oldest unfolded commit reaches the fold age (every 20 minutes for a commit every 10 minutes), and each fold adds a small run for compaction.
- Maintaining a namespace that nobody has claimed costs a claim (about 200 ms). It leaves a fence of at most 572 bytes that collection never deletes, a claim manifest that collection deletes after 24 hours, and a higher epoch, so every other process's cached head reloads at its next check. A namespace that stops changing stops needing work, so these claims follow activity.
- Without placement, two servers that act on one namespace displace each other, so a deployment of several servers needs placement to maintain one store. The retry absorbs one displacement per request, the reply and the displacement metric show the operator, and each displaced request or misrouted install costs a claim, a fence and the displaced session's drain. A claim against a busy writer takes longer than 200 ms, because its manifest put races the writer's manifests. Against a writer that published about 4.7 manifests a second, one takeover took 1.5 seconds (`discovery-fix-on-s3-20261126`).
- The tail of a crashed writer, or of an embedded session that goes quiet, stays unfolded until a process claims the namespace. Readers replay it, and a fork or a merge that needs it folded claims the namespace.

## What goes

- **The floor object**, with its rules in [Advancing the retention floor](../specs/format.md#101-advancing-the-retention-floor): the put without the epoch, the check after its put, the retry up to 1,024 attempts, the session's queued floor object and its fallback, the floor of a view computed from the tail, the fold's maximum over floor objects, the collision with a floor object in [Publication and group commit](../specs/format.md#63-publication-and-group-commit), and the floor field of [WAL records](../specs/format.md#a5-wal-records).
- **The maintainer role and rebuilding over another process's publication**: the fold's reload of its tail over another writer's claim in [Publishing a manifest](../specs/format.md#72-publishing-a-manifest), the compaction's retry over another process's compaction and its "lost every attempt" outcome in [Streaming compaction](../specs/format.md#104-streaming-compaction), the extension entry's `superseded` and `rebuilt` outcomes, the position's walk over manifests of its own epoch that another process published and its drop at a manifest that folds past its tip in [Replaying the visible WAL](../specs/format.md#42-replaying-the-visible-wal), the HEAD walk before a compaction unit plans, and the dead fence's second fence in [Writer ownership](../specs/format.md#61-writer-ownership). Applying a change to the position's manifest stays, because every lane item does it.
- **Reloading and retrying a manifest publication**: the publication function's loop that builds again on the manifest that won a lost put, the fold's and the deletion's reloads of the newest manifest (a fork's read of the head keeps its reload), and the window change, extension entry and compaction publications that run beside the session.
- **The idle fold trigger**, timed from the newest commit, which the fold age replaces.
- **The server modes in which a process maintains namespaces it doesn't write**: `maintain_only`, the scheduler of `serve_only` and `disabled` that leaves folds to another process, and a grep host that maintains the index of namespaces that another server writes. The one setting `writer_builds` replaces them, beside the choices to serve the maintenance API group and to sweep the store.
- **The snapshot's shared mode of the fold lock**, and a deletion that waits for the fold lock while it keeps the lane.
- **Tests of rival publishers**: the fold race tests, the cross-process fold, compaction and advance tests, and the floor object tests.

## Alternatives considered

- **A writer plus one separately claimed compactor.** A second epoch removes rival compactions, but it needs its own claim and fence and a rule for a compaction that runs while the writer folds, and the floor advance still has to be ordered against commits, which only the writer's pause of its own commit queue does.
- **One fold of the whole tail while commits are paused.** An advance or a deletion would keep the lane and pause the commit queue while it folds the whole tail, which saves one manifest, but commits would wait for that whole fold (2.7 s at p50 for 256 WAL objects of small inline files in `floor-advance-contention-20261126`).
- **A marker in the WAL from another process.** A floor object orders an advance against restores without the epoch, but it loses the next WAL number to a busy writer, has to be kept and checked after its put, and breaks the fence's check as [The problem](#the-problem) shows.
- **One queue for WAL puts and manifest puts.** It would save the pause of the commit queue for the tombstone and the stop of both queues after an unknown outcome, but every manifest put would take its time from commits. In a probe on S3 that sent both kinds of put through one lock, a writer of 60 KiB commits whose process published about 5 manifests a second made 16.5% fewer commits a second (`lane-cost-on-s3-20261127`), an upper bound because the probe keeps lost manifest puts.
- **Asking the writer through an address in the manifest.** An address can go stale, so a process needs a timer or a liveness check to know when to stop asking and claim, and changing the address is another publication.
- **A lease.** A process would be the writer until a lease expires, renewing it with a write every few seconds. That costs a write per period per namespace, needs a rule for work that outlives the lease, and makes the writer depend on clocks, while a paused writer still needs the epoch to fence it.
